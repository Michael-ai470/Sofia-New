# E2 · T3 — Screening Questions

Replaces `## Task T3 — Screening questions` in the E2 engine module.

```
## Task T3 — Screening questions

Eight to ten questions that separate the candidates who can do this job from \
the candidates who can describe it.

This is screening, not interviewing. It runs in fifteen to twenty minutes, on \
a call or a form, often conducted by someone who does not do the job \
themselves. Every question must be answerable in about ninety seconds, and \
must be judgeable by a person who is not an expert in the work. A question \
that needs expertise to mark is an interview question, not a screening one.

### Order them by what they cost

Cheapest disqualifier first. A question that takes thirty seconds and rules \
out a third of applicants belongs before one that takes three minutes and \
rules out nobody — hard prerequisites, availability, the non-negotiable \
capability the role stands on.

Ending a conversation at minute two respects both people. Discovering the \
same thing at minute eighteen wastes an afternoon across a shortlist.

### Each question carries four fields

- THE QUESTION, worded exactly as it should be asked. The screener reads it \
aloud as written; do not leave them to phrase it.
- WHAT A STRONG ANSWER CONTAINS. The specifics to listen for — a mechanism, a \
number, a named constraint, a decision they made and why. Not sentiment.
- WHAT A WEAK ANSWER SOUNDS LIKE. Write out the plausible non-answer in full. \
This is the field that earns its place: a screener who has read the fluent \
empty answer once will recognise it live, which is exactly when there is no \
time to work it out.
- THE FOLLOW-UP when the first answer is vague. This is usually the question \
that does the real work, because the first answer is usually rehearsed and \
the second rarely is.

### Ask what has happened, not what would

"Walk me through the last time you..." surfaces evidence. "What would you do \
if..." surfaces imagination, and rewards the candidate who interviews well \
over the candidate who works well.

Reach for a hypothetical only where the situation is one almost no candidate \
will have faced. Where you do, say in the strong-answer field that you are \
listening for reasoning, not for the right answer.

At least one question must probe the largest risk in this role — the thing \
that, if the hire turns out to be wrong about it, costs the most to discover \
in month four. Name that risk in one line before the question, so the \
screener knows why it is there.

### What not to ask

Never a question answerable by reading the job description back. "Why do you \
want to work here" and "what interests you about this role" produce prepared \
answers that separate nobody.

Never anything that probes a protected characteristic, including by the \
indirect routes: where someone is originally from, what year they graduated, \
whether they plan to have children, their marital or health status, how they \
will arrange childcare, their nationality beyond the right to work.

Where the role carries a genuine requirement — a shift pattern, travel, a \
licence, the right to work — ask about the requirement directly and never \
about the circumstance behind it. "Are you able to work Saturdays" is a fair \
screening question. "Do you have young children" is the same question asked \
unlawfully, and it produces a worse answer.

Ask every candidate the same questions in the same order. Screening only \
compares candidates if it puts the same thing to each of them; a conversation \
that wanders differently per person produces impressions, not a comparison.

### What screening cannot do

Some requirements cannot be assessed by asking. Where the role depends on \
something only visible in the work itself — the quality of someone's writing, \
whether they can actually build the thing, how they handle a real dataset — \
say so rather than manufacturing a question that pretends to test it.

Close with one short section: what this screen cannot tell you, and what \
would — a work sample, a technical stage, a reference check. Two or three \
lines. A screener who knows the limits of the screen makes better decisions \
than one who believes it covered everything.

### Before you return

Check silently. Could each question be answered in ninety seconds. Could a \
non-expert judge the answer using the strong and weak fields alone. Is any \
question answerable from the job description. Does any question touch a \
protected characteristic by any route. Is the hardest disqualifier early \
rather than late. Does every question have a follow-up that is more specific \
than the original.
```
