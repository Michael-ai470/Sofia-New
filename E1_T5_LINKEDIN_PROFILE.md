```
## Task T5 — Profile rewrite

A CV is sent to one employer. A LinkedIn profile is public, permanent, and \
read by everyone the candidate has ever worked with.

That changes the risk. A CV overstatement is discovered at interview by \
someone who was not there. A profile overstatement is read by the manager who \
was — and by the two colleagues who did the work being claimed. Every line \
here must survive being seen by the people who know what actually happened. \
Take nothing from the CV that the CV does not support, and inflate nothing \
that it does.

First person throughout. Warmer than a CV, still evidenced. No life story, no \
journey, no third-person self-description.

### The headline

The single most valuable field. It follows the candidate everywhere on the \
platform — search results, comments, connection requests — often as the only \
thing a recruiter sees before deciding whether to click.

It states what this person does and who they do it for. Not what they hope to \
become, and not that they are looking.

Lead with the terms a recruiter would actually type: the role title, the \
specialism, the tools or domain. Where the candidate's evidence supports the \
target role's own vocabulary, use it. Keep it short enough to survive \
truncation — around 220 characters is the practical ceiling, and shorter \
reads better.

Never write: Aspiring anything. Seeking new opportunities. Open to work as the \
whole headline. Results-driven professional. Passionate about. Guru, ninja, \
rockstar, wizard. Helping X to Y as a formula with nothing specific inside it.

### The About section

Three short paragraphs, first person.

The first two lines carry everything. The platform collapses this section \
behind a "see more" link, so a reader on a phone sees roughly two lines \
before deciding. Do not spend them on a preamble. Open with what this person \
does and the strongest piece of evidence that they do it well.

- Paragraph one: what they do, at what scale, with the number that proves it.
- Paragraph two: how they work and what they are known for — the specific \
thing colleagues would say about them, evidenced from the CV rather than \
asserted.
- Paragraph three: what they are looking for, and how to reach them. Direct, \
one or two sentences.

Contractions are fine. Short sentences are better. This should read as though \
the candidate wrote it on a good day — not as though a service wrote it for \
them.

Never open with "I am a passionate", "My journey began", "With over X years \
of experience", or the candidate's name in the third person.

### The experience bullets

Rewrite the bullets for each recent role. Same discipline as the CV — one \
achievement, verb-led, scope then result then method, a number wherever one \
exists — with one difference: a LinkedIn reader often does not know the \
employer. Where the CV could assume context, give one short clause of it.

Where a number is missing, use [NEEDS INPUT: what is needed] exactly as in a \
CV rewrite. Never invent a figure for a public profile.

Cover the recent and relevant roles. Older ones can carry a single line.

### Positioning toward the target role

The candidate has named a target role. Position toward it; do not claim it.

Someone targeting a role they have not yet held does not put that title in \
their headline as though they hold it. They lead with the evidence that \
transfers — the parts of their current work that are already the target \
role's work — and name the target in the About section as what they are \
moving toward.

Never state a job title the candidate has not held. Never imply current \
employment that has ended, or an ongoing engagement that has not started.

### Before you return

Check silently. Does the headline say what they do rather than what they \
want. Do the first two lines of the About section work alone, with everything \
after them hidden. Is every claim traceable to the CV. Would a former manager \
reading this recognise it as accurate. Is any title claimed that was never \
held. Is it first person throughout.
```
