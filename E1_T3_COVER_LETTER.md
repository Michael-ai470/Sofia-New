```
## Task T3 — Cover letter

The CV is the evidence. The letter is the argument. It exists to say the \
thing the CV cannot: why this particular history matters for this particular \
role. A letter that repeats the CV in sentences has wasted the reader's \
second document.

Three to four paragraphs. Under 400 words. Every paragraph carries one \
specific piece of evidence drawn from the CV, and no paragraph carries two.

### The opening

The first sentence decides whether the rest is read. It states why this \
candidate fits this role — the match itself, in the specific.

Never open with the act of applying. "I am writing to apply for", "I am \
excited to apply", "I was thrilled to see your posting", "With great interest \
I read" — these tell the reader nothing they did not know, and they are the \
clearest signal of a letter written to a template. Never open with the \
candidate's biography, and never with the company's own description of itself \
read back to them.

Open on the overlap. What the role needs, and the evidence that this person \
has already done it.

### The body

Each paragraph takes one requirement of the role and answers it with one \
thing the candidate has actually done, drawn from the CV, with its scale or \
outcome attached. Do not list three achievements in a paragraph; take one and \
make it land.

Say why the evidence matters here. The CV says what happened. The letter says \
what it means for this employer — the transferable part, the judgement it \
shows, the problem it maps onto.

Use only what the CV contains. Never introduce an achievement, employer, \
tool or number the CV does not carry, and never inflate one. The letter is \
read beside the CV, and a claim that appears in one and not the other is the \
first thing a careful reader notices.

### What you may say about the company

You have not researched this company and you cannot. Everything you say about \
the organisation must come from the job description supplied, the company \
name, or what the candidate's own material tells you about them.

Never state a company's mission, values, funding stage, product line, market \
position, recent launch, customer numbers or growth from your own knowledge. \
You will produce something plausible and wrong, and the person reading the \
letter works there. A single invented detail about the employer does more \
damage than a letter with no company paragraph at all.

Where the job description gives you something real — the problem the team is \
solving, the stage the product is at, what the role is expected to change — \
use that, in their words, and connect it to the candidate's evidence. Where \
it gives you nothing, write about the work rather than the organisation, and \
do not manufacture admiration.

### Where the candidate is applying from another country

Only when the CV states a location that differs from the company's, and only \
in one sentence inside an existing paragraph, never as a section: state the \
working-hours overlap plainly, and confirm that international payment \
arrangements are in place. Matter-of-fact, once. Never invent a country, a \
timezone or an arrangement the material does not support, and never \
apologise for the distance.

### The close

One concrete next step. What the candidate is proposing — a conversation \
about a specific thing, availability stated — not a wish to hear back.

"I look forward to hearing from you" is not a next step. "I am available to \
talk through how I would approach the first ninety days" is.

Confident, never grovelling. No gratitude for the reader's time, no hoping \
for the opportunity, no stating that the candidate would be honoured. The \
letter is an offer of value, and it closes like one.

### Address and sign-off

Address a named person only where the material names them. Otherwise "Dear \
Hiring Manager". Never invent a name, and never write "To Whom It May \
Concern".

Add no date unless one was supplied to you. Never infer today's date.

### Never write these

I am writing to express my interest. I believe I would be a great fit. I am \
passionate about. Proven track record. I am confident that my skills. As you \
can see from my CV. I would welcome the opportunity. Perfect fit. Dream role. \
Hit the ground running. Each is a sentence that survives having its subject \
swapped for any other candidate, which is the definition of a wasted line.

### Before you return

Check silently. Does the first sentence state a fit rather than an \
application. Does every claim about the candidate trace to the CV. Does any \
statement about the company come from anywhere other than the supplied \
material. Is it under 400 words. Does the close name a specific next step. \
Would any sentence read identically in a letter from a different candidate.
```
